RIP Bourdieu, you would have loved "Filter Bubbles"

November 23, 2026

In 2021, a young artist named Zach Silberberg in New York City posted what would later become a meme to Twitter:

RIP Pavlov, you would have loved notifications. Zach was more spot on than he realized. Inspired by this framing, I'm starting a short series here discussing how social science plays into the kinds of traps that have been set for us online--sometimes purposefully, sometimes unintentionally.

Today, it's all about a French social scientist and what he had to say about "preferences"... which explains how our internet has splintered under our own apparent "tastes."

The fact is, that in a digital world that we think of as deeply individuated, we're actually living in a nightmarish house of mirrors built to reflect and reinforce our existing social divisions. The social disorders we have seen spread online-- our inability to talk to each other, our personalized filter bubbles-- is not just predicted by social science. All of it was built upon social science.

Why we like what we like

In his 1979 landmark book, Distinction: A Social Critique of the Judgement of Taste, French sociologist Pierre Bourdieu explains that we don't just happen to like what we like. Instead something as personal as "taste" is taught, instructed, inculcated.

Taste is instilled in us from an early age. We acquire our way of being in the world from our social environment, our schools and our parents, our grandparents, our social context. And this way of being in the world (he calls it 'habitus') comes to us already shaped by existing social distinctions.

Writing in 1970's France, the social distinction that mattered to him was all about social class, especially inherited wealth. Today, we might also add other categories: race, gender, sexual orientation, geography, and more

In other words, we learn what our preferences are based on our social class and positioning in society. Grow up in a certain neighborhood with well-off parents, and you learn how to appreciate tennis, oil painting, and classical music. Those apparent "tastes" actually reflect your social formation in a cultural setting.

Taste Keeps us Unequal

Because social class pre-determines and shapes taste, depending on your degree of cultural fluency with these high status tastes ("cultural capital") you can convert this knowledge into actual advancement for yourself and your peers ("social capital.") You look like the right kind of person, with the right networks, and the right fit. People who don't have this cultural capital, can't access those same opportunities.

This is one of the many ways in which inequalities are reproduced across generations.

It is also one of the many ways that the ruling classes maintain control over the lower classes. By patrolling who has access to which kinds of cultural capital, you control who has accesss to social capital and advancement. This is how you limit social mobility, even in a society dedicated to egalitarianism. Not through formal rules, but by informal cultural codes that maintain elitism in society, based on who knows what arcana about "the right" kinds of art, music, cuisine, and sport. 

It gets more insidious. Taste is also how people in charge maintain the illusion that the hierarchy between social categories of people is somehow natural, not social. We don't have to go back to the 1970's to see this in action. A viral chart produced in 2010 by a Caltech student purports to show "music that makes you dumber," where the author relates stated preference for Beethoven versus Beyoncé to indicators of presumed intelligence like SAT scores.

Of course, thanks to Bourdieu, we know that social position influences both musical tastes and access to educational resources that might improve an SAT score. The chart merely shows us that Bourdieu's theories are alive and well. However, it's more convenient for those at the top of the hierarchy if we believe that their superior position is due to inherent, natural advantages instead of due to instructed social distinctions masquarading as personal preference.

There's more -- 600 pages more in the English translation at least. But the basic gist of Bourdieu's theory of taste is that we need to stop thinking of preferences and tastes as deeply personal, and come to see them as deeply social instead. Tastes and preferences reflect the distinctions of the social world we live in. Taste-based distinction also helps elites to perpetuate class distinctions, instead of helping everyday people to overcome them.

And all this helps us understand just what happened when we launched the preference-based advertising regime of the personal data economy online.

'Preference'-based Advertising -- and Content

Like Bourdieu, marketers know a lot about social distinctions. To manage a brand or nudge people toward a sale, advertisers look for target groups that occupy a specific social position -- income, race, gender, etc.-- and then sell to them with ads tailored for that group. In the old days, that used to be buy-outs in only particular newspapers and magazines. By 2011, ad placement became automated, just-in-time, delivered directly to your screen, all targeted based on vast stores of personal data that infered (you guessed it) personal preferences.

When you load a website, systems run by either Meta or Google (everything has consolidated in this market) select exactly the right ad for you, the one you'd be most likely to click on based on your prior inferred preferences. Then they display the ad just in time to your eyeballs as you scroll past the page, making money for the tech company. This is preference-based advertising. A seemingly-benign way to steer people toward the advertising and content imbibing that will make the most money.

But thanks to Bourdieu, we know that those preferences are not just preferences.  They are social cues, indicators of social status and positioning. And they are anything but benign.

Identifying our individual tastes is the same as having a divining rod for finding those vast hidden social inequalities of our day. Wealth disparities. Gender inequities. Racial inequalities. Massive wedges and separations in which all of us see only what our taste-group wants to see. Preference based algorithms identify the boundaries between these social groups, and then wedge them further apart.

Unfortunately, preference-based algorithms have moved far beyond ads. They are also used for content, for news, for videos online, and for all kinds of other information streams and services. What you might want to watch on Netflix. What news you'd be interested in. Which TikTok or YouTubevideos to serve up next. What you see is what the system has inferred you would like to see, based on your taste, your preferences. Ultimately, based on determining your social standing -- and keeping you there.

Trapped in Filter Bubbles for Social Status

So what happened when Bourdieu met the Data Economy? When an entire machinery bent on identifying individual "tastes" was rolled out to deliver "preference-based" content, across the world?

Our first indication came as early as 2011, when internet critic Eli Pariser wrote a book about this phenomenon that he called "the Filter Bubble." He noted that even then, the Internet's grand sorting and algorithmic machinery was showing us all different versions of reality. We all lived in a bubble filtered to show us each different products, different information, different realities.

These filter bubbles, however, are not individual. They are socially structured. They contain all the distinctions that our societies inculcate in people. It's not just that we all see different realities. It's that we see different realities based on our pre-existing social groups. Each social group therefore sees its own reality, and no one else's.

Black teenager in Atlanta? You'll see x,y,z but never a,b,c. White woman in New Jersey? You'll see a,b,c but never x,y,z.  All those machine learning algorithms simply found our social groups through identifying tastes. They then proceeded to conflate taste with social identity, social markers like race and class that have nothing to do with who we truly are.

This makes social distinctions even more pronounced. 

What's at Stake

Taste-based filter bubbles diminish people's ability to hold, acquire, or wield any other forms of cultural capital. You'll never discover the artists, the sports, the news, the activities that are preferred by other groups of people.

That doesn't mean that the only way to achieve upward social mobility is to take blue collar workers to the Louvre (this has been hotly debated since Bourdieu's time). But the inability to learn anything about what people outside your own social milieu like, means you will be unable to wield that knowledge for social advancement. That's the end of the road for social mobility, to say nothing of the empathy and common understanding necessary to hold societies together.

It also means that distinctive "tastes" can now be controlled directly through what appear to be "personalized preferences". With AI infiltrating our media systems, now everyone in the middle and lower classes can trend toward a tasteless mean. This makes upward mobility even more challenging. Even if people develop what appear to be all the trappings of "good taste" delivered to them online, their AI-generated sense of what is fashionable or informative will not grant them access to social status or power.

And whom does all this serve? The elite, of course. Bourdieu thought that it was in the best interest of the wealthy to make it seem like their upper crust taste was simply the result of a natural predeliction toward the superior. A cultivated palette. A truly discerning eye, or ear. These socially inculcated distinctions translated directly into class differentiators. Like music taste and SAT scores, these tastes appeared to merely be natural distinctions between the elite and the lower class. 

Keeping others out, policing others from developing these cultural fluencies, concentrates power at the top, and ensures it stays there.

What the Internet Could Yet Be

The Internet was born in a time that Bourdieu would have recognized, a moment when scholars and governments were obsessed with leveling distinctions in society. The internet was supposed to be a grand equalizer. A sharer of information. An opportunity for social mobility. A way to move up in society through exposure to how other people lived, what they thought, what they liked and knew.

Preference-based filter bubbles changed all that. Identifying our tastes was necessary to sell us more things, to send catered content to our eyeballs. It appeared to be a win-win for consumers, who then only saw what they wanted to see. 

But because taste is neither neutral nor personal, instead of building something that could break down walls, we created machines that identify and uphold boundaries between us. Boundaries between social classes, races, genders, and many other distinctions besides, all based on identification of our "taste."

People crow about how well they have trained "my algorithm" to show them only what they like. They don't recognize that training the systems to zero in on their "likes" only enhances our existing social disinctions, makes them stronger, more reinforced. Even more difficult to surmount or see past.

We feel like we have found "our people" online. We feel like we're not alone, that someone out there understands us. But we have actually increased the distance and distinctions between ourselves and people in other social groups. Instead of building understanding or bridging divides, we dig ever deeper into our social position, ever further from others, and ever further from access to the kind of power that can induce societies to change. 

This is one of the (many) reasons why I am so firmly, adamently against the data economy and personal preference detection. Naturalizing social distinctions is a great way to ensure that societies get more racist, more sexist, more classist, more discriminatory. It is also a great way to ensure that people --whoever they are, wherever they are born -- will never reach their full potential because they will have been put in a tiny box, from which there is no visible escape.  And it is a great way to ensure that throughout, elites maintain control over the hierarchy of our various "tastes" -- and use their position to stay on top.
